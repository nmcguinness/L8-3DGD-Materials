Noise Types and Octaves

How the balance of large and small detail in a noise decides whether it looks like static, clouds or hills, and how to build and tune that balance in a material.

1. Framing Question

Why does some noise look like TV static, some like clouds, and some like rolling hills - and how do we choose?

All three are random, so randomness is not what separates them. What separates them is how much of the variation happens over large distances and how much over small ones. Once you can see and set that balance, choosing a noise for rock, fog, terrain or dithering becomes a decision with a reason behind it.

5. Core Ideas

5.1 Random versus coherent noise

Random noise gives every sample an independent value, so neighbouring pixels have nothing in common: that is TV static. Coherent noise changes smoothly, so neighbouring samples are similar and the result has shapes. Almost every texture-like use of noise needs coherence, because surfaces in the world have structure at some size.

5.2 Frequency and amplitude

Any signal can be described as a sum of waves. A wave's frequency is how many times it repeats across the image, and its amplitude is how strongly it varies. Low frequencies make large shapes; high frequencies make fine grain. Describing noise by how much amplitude it has at each frequency is what makes different noises comparable.

5.3 The power spectrum

The power spectrum plots the strength (amplitude squared) of each frequency. On log-log axes, many useful noises are straight lines, and the slope, beta, describes the noise in one number: power is proportional to f to the power beta. View A3 measures that slope from the image, so you can check a noise rather than trust its name.

5.5 Why blue noise is preferred for dithering and sampling

Dithering and stochastic effects add a small random offset per pixel to hide banding or approximate transparency. White noise has as much low-frequency power as high, so its errors clump into blotches the eye picks out. Blue noise puts its power at high frequencies, so its errors are spread evenly and are easy to blur away, which is exactly what temporal anti-aliasing and a slight blur do. The pattern looks finer for the same amount of noise.

5.6 Value noise and gradient noise

Value noise places a random value at each point of a grid and blends between them. It is cheap, but its peaks and troughs sit on grid points, so the grid shows. Gradient noise, such as Perlin noise and its successor simplex noise, places a random slope at each grid point instead, so the value is zero at every grid point and the features fall between them. That hides the grid and gives rounder, more even shapes, at a little more cost.

5.7 Fractal Brownian motion

fBm builds detail at many scales from one coherent noise by adding copies of it. Each copy is an octave. Two numbers control the stack. Lacunarity multiplies the frequency from one octave to the next, usually 2. Gain, also called persistence, multiplies the amplitude, usually 0.5. The result has large shapes from the first octave and fine grain from the last.

5.8 How gain shapes the spectrum

Each octave doubles the frequency and multiplies the amplitude by the gain, so across octaves amplitude falls as f to the power log2(gain). Power is amplitude squared, so the spectral slope is about 2 log2(gain). With the usual gain of 0.5, that gives a slope of -2, a brown-like result: smooth, hill-like shapes. A gain of about 0.71 gives a slope of -1, a pink-like result with more fine detail. Raising the gain is therefore the same decision as choosing a noise colour.

5.9 Turbulence and ridged variants

Turbulence adds the absolute value of each octave instead of the signed value. Folding every octave at zero creates creases where the noise crosses zero, which reads as billowing smoke or fire. Ridged noise inverts the folded value and squares it, so the creases become sharp crests: mountain ridges, veins and cracks.

6. Common Pitfalls

SymptomCauseFix
A visible square grid in the noiseValue noise, whose features sit on grid pointsUse gradient or simplex noise, or rotate each octave's coordinates
The pattern repeats across a large surfaceThe noise is a tiling texture, or every octave uses the same lattice offsetUse world-position input with large-scale variation, and offset each octave
Washed-out, grey resultMany octaves at high gain add many independent values, which average towards the middleRemap the output range, or use fewer octaves or a lower gain
Shimmer or sparkle in the distanceOctaves above half a cycle per pixel cannot be shown and aliasLimit the octave count, or fade octaves out by distance with derivatives
Values below 0 or above 1 clip to flat black or whiteThe summed amplitude exceeds the expected rangeDivide by the amplitude sum, then remap to the range you need
Dithered gradients show blotches or bandingWhite noise dither, whose low-frequency power forms clumpsUse a blue noise texture for dithering
