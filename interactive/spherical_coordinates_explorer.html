Spherical Coordinates Explorer

A direction in 3D needs three numbers in Cartesian form but only two angles in spherical form. Those two angles are what let us wrap a flat texture around a sphere and squeeze a normal into two texture channels.

Why r sinθ? Drop P straight down onto the XY plane. Its shadow sits at distance r sinθ from the origin, and φ spins that shadow around Z exactly like 2D polar coordinates. Height above the plane is r cosθ.

Horizontal squeeze

Pole pinching. Every row of the texture has the same number of texels, but the circle it wraps around has circumference 2π sinθ. Near the poles a whole row is crushed into a tiny ring. Switch to Circles and turn the globe to look down on a pole.
The seam. At φ = 0° u jumps from 1 back to 0. The checker's hue jumps there too. In a shader that jump wrecks the derivatives used for mip selection, which shows up as a thin line of the smallest mip.

HLSL: direction to lat-long UV (Custom node, input Dir, output Float 2)

float3 d = normalize(Dir);
float  u = atan2(d.y, d.x) / (2.0 * PI);   // -0.5..0.5
u = frac(u);                               // wrap to 0..1
float  v = acos(clamp(d.z, -1.0, 1.0)) / PI; // 0 at +Z, 1 at -Z
return float2(u, v);

Packing a normal into two channels

A unit normal has only two degrees of freedom, so two 8-bit channels can hold it. Tangent-space normals all point out of the surface (z ≥ 0), so θ only needs 0° to 90°. Drag on the disc to pick a normal; the colour shows the angular error after quantisation at every direction.

Why the spherical scheme cannot be filtered. Two neighbouring texels hold φ = 358° and φ = 2°, stored as R = 0.994 and R = 0.006. Bilinear filtering averages them to 0.500, which decodes to φ = 180°: a normal pointing the opposite way. Mip-maps fail the same way. XY and hemisphere octahedral have no wrap, so they filter safely.
What Unreal does by default. A texture set to Normalmap compression is stored as BC5: X and Y only, with Z rebuilt in the shader. That is the XY scheme above. A Texture Sample with its Sampler Type set to Normal rebuilds Z for you; the DeriveNormalZ node does the same job when you store X and Y yourself, for example in two channels of a packed mask. Common pitfalls
The bugs that turn up in student shaders every year.

  
    
    
  • Swapped names. Physics texts use θ for the polar angle; many maths texts use φ. Check which angle is measured from the pole, not which letter is used.
    • 
    
  • atan2 argument order. HLSL, C# and C++ take atan2(y, x). Excel's ATAN2 takes (x, y). Swapping them mirrors the azimuth about the 45° line.
    • 
    
  • Negative azimuth. atan2 returns −π to π. Divide by 2π and you get −0.5 to 0.5; wrap with frac or add 2π to negatives before using it as u.
    • 
    
  • NaN from acos. After normalising, z can be 1.0000001. acos of that is NaN, which renders black. Clamp or saturate first.
    • 
    
  • Undefined φ at the poles. At θ = 0° or 180° every azimuth gives the same point. Code that divides by sinθ will blow up there.
    • 
    
  • Seam lines. The u wrap at φ = 0° spikes the screen-space derivatives and selects the lowest mip. Fix it with SampleGrad using derivatives from an unwrapped u, or sample without mips.
    • 
  


  
Check your understanding

  
Predict first, then confirm with the stations above, then open the answer.

  

    
A direction points straight down. What are θ and φ, and where is it in the lat-long texture?
      
θ = 180°, and φ can be anything. The entire bottom row of the texture (v = 1) maps to that single point. Try the −Z preset, then drag the φ slider.

    
At θ = 30°, how much narrower is a texel on the sphere than at the equator?
      
sin 30° = 0.5, so it is half as wide: squeezed by a factor of 2. Station 2 shows this as the horizontal squeeze.

    
Why divide θ by 90° rather than 180° when packing tangent-space normals?
      
Tangent-space normals never point into the surface, so θ never exceeds 90°. Dividing by 90° spreads all 256 levels over the range actually used, doubling the polar precision from about 0.71° to 0.35° per step at 8 bits.

    
Which packing scheme has its worst error at the horizon, and why?
      
XY + derive Z. Near the rim z = √(1 − x² − y²) is very steep, so a one-step rounding of x or y produces a large change in z. Lower the bits to 5 to see it clearly.

    
Viewed from above in Unreal, does φ increase clockwise or anticlockwise?
      
Clockwise. Unreal is left-handed: looking down from +Z, +X is up the screen and +Y is to the right, so turning from +X to +Y is clockwise. Switch handedness in station 1 and look from above to compare.
