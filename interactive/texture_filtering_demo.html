Texture Filtering: From Texels to Anisotropy

An interactive guide to how the GPU turns a UV coordinate into a colour, and why that choice decides whether distant surfaces shimmer, blur or stay crisp.

1. Framing Question

Why does a texture that looks sharp up close shimmer or blur in the distance?

Up close, many screen pixels share one texel, and the question is how to fill the gaps between texels. In the distance, one screen pixel covers many texels, and the question becomes which of them to show. The two problems need different answers, and the sampler settings you choose in Unreal decide which answer you get.

    4. Core Ideas

    4.1 Texels and the pixel footprint

    A texture is a grid of texels. The GPU shades screen pixels, not texels, so every pixel asks the sampler for the colour at one UV coordinate. Project the pixel's square back onto the texture and you get its footprint: the patch of texels that pixel really covers. Every filtering choice is an attempt to summarise that footprint with as few texel reads as possible.

    4.2 Magnification and minification

    When the footprint is smaller than one texel, the texture is being magnified: many pixels share one texel, and the sampler must invent values between texels. When the footprint covers many texels, the texture is being minified: one pixel must stand for many texels, and reading just one of them throws the rest away. Sharpness up close is a magnification question. Shimmer in the distance is a minification question.

    4.3 Nearest-neighbour filtering

    Nearest filtering returns the single texel whose centre is closest to the UV. It costs one read. Magnified, it shows hard-edged blocks, which suits pixel art. Minified, it picks an essentially arbitrary texel from the footprint, and as the camera moves that choice changes from frame to frame, so the surface sparkles and crawls. That flicker is aliasing.

    4.4 Bilinear filtering

    Bilinear filtering reads the four texels around the UV and blends them by distance. The weights are the products of the fractional offsets, so they always sum to 1. It makes magnified textures smooth instead of blocky. It does not fix minification, because four texels are still far too few to stand for a footprint that covers hundreds.

    4.5 Mipmaps

    A mip chain stores the texture again at half size, then quarter size, down to one texel, each level averaged from the one above. The sampler measures the footprint with screen-space derivatives and picks the level where one texel is about the size of one pixel. The averaging was done in advance, so a distant pixel reads a texel that already summarises its whole footprint. The cost is one third more memory.

    4.6 Trilinear filtering

    Choosing a single mip level per pixel leaves visible lines across a floor where the level changes. Trilinear filtering does a bilinear read in the two nearest levels and blends between them by the fractional level, so the change is gradual. That is eight texel reads per pixel. Turn on the mip tint in the demo and compare "Bilinear, nearest mip" with "Trilinear" to see the bands appear and disappear.

    4.7 Anisotropic filtering

    At a glancing angle the footprint is a long, thin shape: narrow across the view, stretched along it. Trilinear filtering has to pick one level for that shape, and it picks by the longer axis, so it blurs the surface in both directions to avoid aliasing in one. Anisotropic filtering takes several trilinear reads along the long axis at a sharper level, so the floor stays crisp. The cost grows with the number of reads, up to eight texel reads per tap times the anisotropy setting.

    4.8 Procedural textures have no mip chain

    A pattern computed in the shader, such as the checkerboard from floor and frac, is evaluated once at the pixel centre. There is no pre-averaged level to fall back on, so it aliases exactly like a nearest-filtered texture with no mips, however good the sampler settings are. The fix must be written by hand: measure the footprint with derivatives (fwidth, or ddx and ddy) and either average the pattern over it or fade detail towards its average colour. The demo's band-limit option integrates the checkerboard over the footprint exactly. The other fix is to bake the pattern into a texture, which gives it a real mip chain.

    5. Common Pitfalls

    SymptomCauseFix
    Distant surfaces shimmer or sparkle as the camera movesMinification with no usable mip chain: mips disabled, nearest or bilinear without mips, or a procedural patternGenerate mips and use trilinear filtering. For procedural patterns, band-limit with derivatives or bake to a texture
    Hard lines across a floor at fixed distancesNearest-mip selection jumps between levelsUse trilinear filtering so levels blend
    Floors and roads blur at glancing anglesOne mip level chosen for a stretched footprintUse anisotropic filtering and check the anisotropy limit
    Colour bleeds across tiles of an atlas, or seams appear at a distanceBilinear reads and mip averaging cross tile bordersAdd padding around tiles, limit the number of mips, or use separate textures or texture arrays
    A thin line runs along a UV seam, such as where a polar or lat-long coordinate wrapsThe UV jumps, so the derivatives spike and the sampler picks the smallest mipSample with explicit derivatives taken from unbroken UVs
    Pixel art looks softBilinear magnification blends neighbouring pixelsUse nearest filtering and no mips for that texture
    Fine lines vanish in the distanceMip averaging dissolves features thinner than a texelUse a sharpening mip generation setting, or make the features thicker in the source art

    7. Exercises

    1. See the shimmer. Choose the Checkerboard and Nearest (no mips), and turn on Animate. Describe what happens near the horizon. Switch to Trilinear and note how the readout's texel reads per pixel change.
    2. Find the bands. Turn on Tint mip levels. Compare Bilinear, nearest mip with Trilinear, and write down the level numbers where the colour changes. Move the probe across a band and record the bilinear weights on each side.
    3. Glancing angles. Set Pitch down to 3 degrees and choose the Text and grid source. Compare Trilinear with Anisotropic at 2x, 8x and 16x. For each, record the anisotropy ratio and the texel reads at the probe, and say which setting you would ship on a mid-range GPU and why.
    4. Stretch: fix a procedural pattern. Choose Procedural in shader and turn on Animate. Explain why changing the filter mode makes no difference. Turn on band-limiting, then use the mip tint to read the estimated level, and explain how the derivative-based fix does the job a mip chain would do.

    8. Reflective Questions

    These are for discussion with your peers and are not graded.

    1. Mipmaps cost a third more memory and trilinear filtering doubles the texel reads. When would you accept shimmer to save either?
    2. Anisotropic filtering is often the first setting lowered on weaker hardware. Which kinds of game would suffer most from that, and why?
    3. A procedural material can be band-limited by hand or baked to a texture. What does each approach cost, and which would you choose for a large terrain?
    4. Texture atlases save draw calls but fight with filtering. How would you decide whether an atlas is worth it for a set of props?

    9. Glossary

    TermMeaning
    AliasingShimmer, crawling or moire caused by sampling a pattern less often than its detail changes
    Anisotropic filteringSeveral trilinear reads along the long axis of a stretched footprint, to keep glancing surfaces sharp
    Band-limitingRemoving detail finer than a pixel can show, before sampling, so it cannot alias
    Bilinear filteringA distance-weighted blend of the four texels around the UV
    DerivativesHow fast a value changes from one pixel to the next, read with ddx, ddy or fwidth. The GPU uses them to measure the footprint
    FootprintThe area of the texture that one screen pixel covers
    MagnificationWhen one texel covers several pixels, as close up
    MinificationWhen one pixel covers several texels, as in the distance
    Mip chainA texture stored at a series of halving sizes, each averaged from the one above
    Mip levelOne size in the mip chain. Level 0 is full size
    Nearest filteringReturning the single closest texel, with no blending
    SamplerThe GPU state that decides how a texture is read: filtering, mip use, anisotropy and wrapping
    TexelOne element of a texture, the texture's equivalent of a pixel
    Texture atlasSeveral images packed into one texture, each in its own region
    Trilinear filteringBilinear reads in the two nearest mip levels, blended by the fractional level
